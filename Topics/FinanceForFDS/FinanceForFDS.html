Performance Measurement

Measuring Returns

Returns are a messy concept

The term “Return” is colloquially abused.

  • Net vs Gross
  • Simple vs Log
  • Realized vs Unrealized
  • Price vs Total
  • etc…

Simple (Daily)

Let’s assume \(t\) is one day

  • 1-Day Net Return (aka Holding Period Yield (HPY)): \(R_{t}=\frac{P_{t}-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}=\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}}-1\)
  • 1-Day Gross Return (aka Holding Period Return (HPR)) \(1+R_{t}=\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}}\)

Simple (Multiperiod)

  • 2-Day Net: \(R_{t}(2)=\frac{P_{t}-P_{t-2}}{P_{t-2}}\)
  • H-Day Net: \(R_{t}(H)=\frac{P_{t}-P_{t-H}}{P_{t-H}}\)
  • H-Day Gross: \(1+R_{t}(H)=(1+R_{t-H+1})\cdots (1+R_{t-1})(1+R_{t})\)
  • H-Day Gross: \(1+R_{t}(H)=\prod_{j=0}^{H-1}(1+R_{t-j})\)

Simple Total Returns (Stocks)

Define \(P\) as price and \(D\) as dividend and consider \(R_{t} = \frac{P_{t}+D_{t}-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}\).

Q: Can you write the Simple Total Return and decompose into two values?

A:

  • \(1+R_{t} = \frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}} + \frac{D_{t}}{P_{t-1}}\)
  • Total Return = “Capital Gains Yield” + “Dividend Yield”

Simple Total Returns (Bonds)

Define \(P\) as the price and \(C\) as the coupon. Assume purchased at par, such that \(P_{t-1}\) equals the face value.

Q: Can you write the Simple Total Return and decompose into two values?

A:

  • \(1+R_{t} = \frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}} + \frac{C_{t}}{P_{t-1}}\)
  • Total Return = “Capital Gains Yield” + “Coupon Rate”

Simple Annualized Returns

Q: What is the meaning of “annualized” return?

A: How much the return would be if continued for an entire year

  • \(R_{t}(H)^{A} = (1+R_{t}(H))^{\frac{\# \text{of periods per year}}{H}}-1\)
  • \(R_{t}(H)^{A} = (\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}})^{\frac{\# \text{of periods per year}}{H}}-1\)

Simple Real Returns

Q: What does “Real” mean? Why use?

A:“Real” means inflation adjusted. Useful for computing growth of assets in terms of buying power.

Q: How compute real returns?

  • \(R_{t}^{Real}(H) = \frac{1+R_{t}(H)}{1+\pi_{t}(H)}-1\)
  • where \(\pi_{t}(H) = CPI_{t}/CPI_{t-H}-1\)
  • Note: CPI is widely used, broad measure of prices, but you could customize. e.g. Price index in your region of the country
  • Note: The horizon for inflation should match the horizon for returns

Shortcoming of Simple Returns

  • Simple Returns are not additive
    • returns on monday + tuesday + … + friday does not equal the week’s return
  • Simple Returns are not symmetric
    • $100 grown 20% is 120. But 120 shrunk by 20% is 96.
  • Our empirical models (e.g. regression) work better with additivity and symmetry
  • Log returns are a common solution
    • Sum of daily log returns is weekly log return
    • $100 grown log return 20% and then shrunk 20% brings you back to $100

From Simple to Log Returns

Let’s focus on daily returns

  • \(r_{t}=ln(1+R_{t})=ln(P_{t}/P_{t-1}) = ln(P_{t})-ln(P_{t-1})\)
  • \(e^{r_{t}}-1=R_{t}\)

MultiPeriod Log Returns

  • \(r_{t}(2) = ln(1+R_{t}(2))=ln(P_{t}/P_{t-2})\)
  • \(r_{t}(2) = ln(\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}}\frac{P_{t-1}}{P_{t-2}})\)
  • \(r_{t}(2) = ln(\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}}) + ln(\frac{P_{t-1}}{P_{t-2}})\)
  • \(r_{t}(2) = r_{t} + r_{t-1}\)

More generally,

\(r_{t}(H) = \sum_{j=0}^{H-1}{r_{t-j}}\)

Annualized Log Returns

\(r_{t}(H)^{A} = (\frac{\text{periods per year}}{H})\times r_{t}(H)\)

Real Log Returns

  • \(r_{t}^{Real} = r_{t}-\pi_{t}^{C}\)
  • where \(\pi^{C}_{t} = ln(1+\pi_{t})\)

Simple or Log?

  • Simple returns are used for performance reporting
  • Log returns are used for statistical modeling

Measuring Risk

Risk is Difficult to Measure

Q: Why is risk so much more difficult to measure than returns?

A: Returns are observed. Risk is not.

We will discuss the meaning of risk soon, but for now we proxy with variance and standard deviation.

  • \(\sigma^{2}_{r_{t}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{(r_{t}-\bar{r})^{2}}\)
  • \(\sigma_{r_{t}} = \sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{r_{t}}}\)
  • Both are commonly referred to as ``volatility”

Symmetric vs Asymmetric Risk Measures

Q: Is treating upside and downside surprises the same always appropriate?

A: Not necessarily.

  • Variance/standard deviation penalize upside and downside surprises equally
  • Investors typically only dislike the downside
  • Asymmetric alternatives: semi-variance, Value-at-Risk (VaR), Conditional VaR (Expected Shortfall)
  • We will build several of these as forecasting targets later in the course

Risk / Return Measures

Common Measures of the Risk/Return Tradeoff (SR)

Sharpe Ratio: \(SR = \frac{R-R^{f}}{\sigma}\), where \(R^{f}\) is the risk free rate

Q: Meaning? Want high or low?

A:

  • Return in excess of risk free per unit of risk
  • We want high

Common Measures of the Risk/Return Tradeoff (Tracking Error)

Tracking Error: \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(R^{p}_{t}-R^{B}_{t})^{2}}\), where \(R^{p}\) is the portfolio return and \(R^{B}\) is the benchmark return.

Q: Meaning? Want high or low?

A:

  • Variability of portfolio return from benchmark
  • An index fund manager wants this low. An active fund manager might permit this to be higher in order to garner excess performance

Other Common Measures

Information Ratio: \(IR = \frac{R^{p}-R^{B}}{\text{Tracking Error}}\)

  • Excess return over benchmark per unit of active (benchmark-relative) risk
  • The Sharpe Ratio of “active” management

Sortino Ratio: \(\frac{R - R^{f}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\min\!\left(0,\; R_t - R^{f}\right)\right]^2}}\)

  • Like Sharpe Ratio, but rewards managers for upside volatility rather than penalizing it

Portfolio Risk and Return

Portfolio Return

Q: What is the return of a portfolio composed of \(N\) assets?

A: \(R^{P}_{t} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}R_{i,t+1}\)

Portfolio Risk

Q: What is the variance of a portfolio composed of \(N\) assets?

A:

  • \(\sigma^{2}_{P,t} = w'Sw\)
  • where \(w\) is (Nx1)
  • and \(S\) is an (NxN) covariance matrix

Portfolio Variance (N=2)

Q: What is the variance of a portfolio of 2 assets?

A:

  • \(\sigma^{2} = w_{A}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{A} + w_{B}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{B}+2\rho_{A,B}w_{A}w_{B}\)

Portfolio Variance (N=3)

Q: What is the variance of a portfolio of 3 assets?

A:

  • \(\sigma^{2} = w_{A}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{A} + w_{B}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{B}+w_{C}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{C}+2\rho_{A,B}w_{A}w_{B} + 2\rho_{A,C}w_{A}w_{C} + 2\rho_{B,C}w_{B}w_{C}\)

Curse of Dimensionality

We will compute portfolio return and variance from historical data for peformance reporting. Moreover, we will construct these objects on a prospective basis for portfolio allocation. Let’s assume for now that the weights are known.

Q: How many parameters do we need to estimate portfolio return of N assets?

A: \(N\) (expected) returns

Q: How many parameters do we need to estimate the portfolio variance of N assets?

A: \(N+ N(N-1)/2\). For a portfolio of 500 assets that is 125,250 parameters (500 variances and 124,750 unique pairwise covariances)

Modern Portfolio Theory

What Is Risk?

Q: What is risk, really?

A:

  • Variance/standard deviation are convenient proxies, not definitions of risk
  • Risk is uncertainty about future outcomes that matter for wealth or wellbeing
  • Not all uncertainty is symmetric, or equally compensated by markets

Systematic vs Idiosyncratic Risk

Q: Are all sources of risk equally important to a diversified investor?

A:

  • Idiosyncratic (unsystematic) risk: firm or asset-specific, can be diversified away
  • Systematic (market) risk: common across assets, cannot be diversified away
  • Markets only compensate investors for bearing systematic risk

Diversification

Q: Why does combining assets reduce risk?

A:

  • Recall the N=2 portfolio variance formula: as long as \(\rho_{A,B}<1\), portfolio std. dev. is less than the weighted average of the individual std. devs.
  • Diversification eliminates idiosyncratic risk; it cannot eliminate systematic risk
  • As \(N\rightarrow\infty\), portfolio variance converges to the average covariance across assets — the systematic risk “floor”

Expected Utility and Risk Aversion

Q: How do we formalize investor preferences over risky outcomes?

A:

  • Expected utility: \(E[U(W)] = \sum_{s}p_{s}U(W_{s})\)
  • Risk aversion: concave utility, \(U''(W)<0\)
  • Certainty equivalent: the certain payoff that delivers the same utility as the risky gamble
  • A risk-averse investor’s certainty equivalent is always below the gamble’s expected value

Mean-Variance Preferences

Q: When can we summarize preferences using just mean and variance?

A:

  • Quadratic utility, or
  • Returns are jointly normal (or elliptically distributed)
  • Under either condition: \(U = E[R_{p}]-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sigma^{2}_{p}\), where \(\lambda\) is the investor’s risk-aversion coefficient

The Efficient Frontier

Q: With many risky assets available, which portfolios are “best”?

A:

  • Minimum-variance frontier: the lowest-variance portfolio achievable for each level of expected return
  • Efficient frontier: the upper portion of that frontier (highest return for each level of variance)
  • Rational, risk-averse investors only ever hold portfolios on the efficient frontier

Adding a Risk-Free Asset

Q: What changes once a risk-free asset is available?

A:

  • Capital Allocation Line (CAL): straight line from \(R_{f}\), tangent to the risky-asset frontier
  • Tangency portfolio: the single risky portfolio touched by that line
  • Two-Fund Separation: every investor holds some mix of the risk-free asset and the same tangency portfolio; only the mix (leverage vs. safety) differs by investor

Portfolio Optimization in Practice

Q: How do we solve for optimal weights?

A:

  • Minimize \(w'Sw\) subject to \(w'\mu=R^{target}\) and \(w'\mathbf{1}=1\)
  • Solved via quadratic programming / Lagrangian methods
  • Inputs (\(\mu\), \(S\)) are estimated with error — “optimization is error maximization” (Michaud)
  • Motivates shrinkage estimators, Black-Litterman, and robust optimization — themes we’ll revisit with ML tools later in the course

Asset Pricing

Pricing Risk Free Assets

Compounding

Q: How much is $100 grown at 5% per year for one year?

A:

  • \(100\times (1.05) = 105\)
  • This is known as compounding
  • $100: Present Value
  • $105: Future Value
  • 5%: Compounding rate
  • \(FV = PV(1+R)\)

Discounting

Q: How much is $105 promised (with certainty) in one year from now worth today?

A:

  • \(FV = PV(1+R)\) \(\rightarrow\) \(PV = FV/(1+R)\)
  • We know FV
  • Our goal is to find PV
  • But we don’t know R
  • R is known as the discount rate
  • The discount rate captures the opportunity cost (i.e. how much return you require to accept the investment)
  • If the investor’s next best option yielded 5%, then \(PV = 105/(1+.05) = 100\)

Coupon Bonds

Q: What is the formula for the PV of a 2yr semi annual coupon bond?

A:

  • \(PV = \frac{C}{1+R} + \frac{C}{(1+R)^{2}} + \frac{C}{(1+R)^{3}} + \frac{C+F}{(1+R)^{4}}\)
  • C: coupon ($)
  • F: face ($)
  • R: periodic discount rate (%)
  • C/F: Coupon Rate (%)

Discount Bond

Q: What is the formula for the PV of a N-yr discount bond?

A:

  • \(PV = \frac{F}{(1+R)^{N}}\)
  • Discount Bonds don’t pay any coupons
  • F: face ($)
  • R: periodic discount rate (%)
  • N: number of periods

Asset Market Equilibrium

Market Risk

  • Q: How use exposure to market fluctuations to determine if a stock is over/undervalued? (note: there are several approaches)
  • A: CAPM
    • First pass: Get beta via time series regression \(r_{t}=a+\beta r^{M}_{t}+u_{t}\), where \(r_{t}\) is the log excess return at time \(t\). Repeat for each stock \(i\) and get \(\hat{\beta}_{i}\)
    • Second pass: Get lambda via cross section regression \(\bar{r}_{i}=\lambda_{0} + \lambda\hat{\beta}_{i}+e_{i}\), where \(r_{i}\) is log excess return for asset \(i\)
    • Use for valuation (see below)

Interpreting the CAPM

Q: What do beta and lambda mean?

A:

  • \(\beta_{i} = \frac{Cov(r_{i},r_{m})}{Var(r_{m})}\): sensitivity of asset \(i\) to market moves (exposure to market risk)
  • \(\lambda\): market price of risk (market risk premium)

The Security Market Line

Q: How do we use CAPM to judge if a stock is over- or under-valued?

A:

  • Security Market Line (SML): y-axis: return vs x-axis: beta
  • Points of the line dictated by CAPM second pass regression. Slope is the \(\lambda\), which for the CAPM is the \(E[r_{m}-r_{f}]\)
  • Plot your subjective expected return. If your subjective expectued return is above the SML, \(\alpha_{i}=E[r^{subjective}]-E[r^{capm}]>0\); then you believe the stock is undervalued (your expectation is greater than what’s priced into the market visa vis CAPM)
  • If your subjective expected return is below the SML, \(\alpha_{i}=E[r^{subjective}]-E[r^{capm}]<0\);then you believe the stock is overvalued (your expectation is less than what’s priced into the market visa vis CAPM)

Factor Risk

Q: What if a single market factor doesn’t fully explain returns?

A:

  • Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT): returns driven by multiple systematic factors
  • \(R_{i} = \alpha_{i}+\beta_{i,1}F_{1}+\beta_{i,2}F_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{i,K}F_{K}+\epsilon_{i}\)
  • Fama-French 3-Factor: Market, SMB (size), HML (value)
  • Fama-French 5-Factor adds RMW (profitability), CMA (investment)
  • Momentum (UMD) often added as a 6th factor
  • Factor models double as risk-attribution tools and as feature sets for return-prediction models

Fixed Income

Duration

Q: How do we measure a bond’s sensitivity to interest rate changes?

A:

  • Macaulay Duration: \(D = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}t\cdot\frac{CF_{t}}{(1+R)^{t}}}{PV}\) — the PV-weighted average time to receive cash flows
  • Modified Duration: \(D_{mod} = \frac{D}{1+R}\)
  • Price sensitivity approximation: \(\frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx -D_{mod}\times\Delta R\)
  • Convexity captures the curvature the linear duration approximation misses

Duration Matching

Q: Why do risk managers care about duration?

A:

  • Immunization: match asset duration to liability duration so a rate move affects both similarly
  • Longer maturity or lower coupon \(\rightarrow\) higher duration; higher coupon \(\rightarrow\) lower duration
  • Zero-coupon (discount) bonds have duration equal to maturity — the maximum duration for a given maturity

Yield Curve

Q: Why is the yield curve typically upward sloping?

A:

  • Expectations: if future short rates are expected to rise, long yields exceed short yields
  • Term/liquidity premium: investors demand compensation for locking up money longer and bearing more rate risk
  • Normal growth expectations + term premium \(\rightarrow\) upward slope, on average

Reading the Curve

Q: What does the shape imply?

A:

  • Steep curve: often associated with expectations of stronger growth/inflation
  • Flat curve: uncertainty or a policy transition
  • Inverted curve (short > long): historically watched as a signal of rising recession risk, since it implies the market expects future rate cuts (weaker growth ahead)
  • Caveat: the curve reflects expectations, term premia, and central bank policy simultaneously — no single indicator is perfect

Equity

Dividend Discount Model

Q: What is a stock worth?

A: Discounted value of future stream of cash flows, which consist of dividends and price at time of sale.

  • \(V_{0} = \frac{E_{t}[D_{t+1}]}{1+k}+\frac{E_{t}[D_{t+2}]}{(1+k)^{2}}+\cdots +\frac{E_{t}[D_{t+H}+P_{H}]}{(1+k)^{H}}\), where \(k\) is the discount rate and \(D\) are dividends.
  • \(V_{0} = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{D_{t}}{(1+k)^{t}}\)

Gordon Growth Model

Q: What if dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate forever?

A:

  • \(V_{0} = \frac{D_{1}}{k-g}\), where \(g\) is the constant dividend growth rate and \(k>g\)
  • Simple, but very sensitive to the assumed \(g\) and \(k\) — small changes in either can swing valuation a lot

P/E Ratio

Q: What is the P/E ratio? How is it measured? How is it used?

A:

  • P/E = Price to Earnings
  • Price is observed
  • Earnings of the company can be historic or expected. Usually for a 4Qtr period.
  • Trailing P/E uses realized past earnings; Forward P/E uses expected (analyst) earnings
  • PEG = P/E \(\div\) expected earnings growth rate — adjusts P/E for differences in growth across stocks
  • “High” P/E implies the stock is expensive
  • “High” might be judged relative to peers, own past, or some subjective measure

Corporate Fundamentals

Income Statement

Q: What does the income statement measure?

A: The flow of revenues and expenses over a period, ending in net income.

  • Revenue (Sales)
  • \(-\) Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) \(=\) Gross Profit
  • \(-\) Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D) \(=\) Operating Income (EBIT)
  • \(-\) Interest Expense \(=\) Pre-Tax Income (EBT)
  • \(-\) Taxes \(=\) Net Income
  • Net Income \(\div\) Shares Outstanding \(=\) EPS

Balance Sheet

Q: What does the balance sheet measure?

A: A snapshot of what a firm owns and owes at a point in time.

  • Assets \(=\) Liabilities \(+\) Shareholders’ Equity
  • Current assets/liabilities: convert to (or come due in) cash within one year
  • Non-current: long-term assets (PP&E, goodwill) and long-term debt
  • Working Capital \(=\) Current Assets \(-\) Current Liabilities

Cash Flow Statement

Q: Why do we need a third statement beyond the income statement and balance sheet?

A: Net income relies on accruals (non-cash items); the cash flow statement reconciles it to actual cash.

  • Operating Cash Flow (CFO): cash generated by the core business
  • Investing Cash Flow (CFI): capex, acquisitions, asset sales
  • Financing Cash Flow (CFF): debt issuance/repayment, dividends, buybacks
  • Free Cash Flow (FCF) \(=\) CFO \(-\) CapEx: cash available to all capital providers

Key Ratios

Q: How do we summarize firm health from these three statements?

A:

Profitability

  • Gross Margin \(=\) Gross Profit / Revenue
  • Net Margin \(=\) Net Income / Revenue
  • ROE \(=\) Net Income / Equity; ROA \(=\) Net Income / Assets

Liquidity

  • Current Ratio \(=\) Current Assets / Current Liabilities
  • Quick Ratio \(=\) (Current Assets \(-\) Inventory) / Current Liabilities

Key Ratios (continued)

Leverage

  • Debt/Equity \(=\) Total Debt / Equity
  • Interest Coverage \(=\) EBIT / Interest Expense

Efficiency

  • Asset Turnover \(=\) Revenue / Assets
  • Inventory Turnover \(=\) COGS / Average Inventory

Macro Drivers

Macro KPI

Q: What are the 4 major macro KPIs?

A:

  • GDP: Gross Domestic Product (measure of aggregate economic activity)
  • UR: Unemployment Rate (fraction of labor force unemployed)
  • CPI: Consumer Price Index (price index for consumer purchases)
  • 10yr: Yield on US Ten Year Treasury (yield to maturity)

Connecting the KPIs

Explaining the Connections

Q: Can you theoretically connect the Macro KPIs?

Okun’s Law

Q: What does Okun’s Law capture?

A:

  • Relationship between labor market and aggregate output
  • Greater demand for production implies greater demand for labor…
  • … if labor supply is unchanged, then …
  • … unemployment rate falls

The Phillips Curve

Q: What tradeoff does the Phillips Curve describe?

A:

  • Short-run: an inverse relationship between inflation and unemployment
  • Intuition: tighter labor markets (low unemployment) push wages and prices up
  • The long-run Phillips Curve is widely viewed as vertical at the “natural rate” of unemployment — no permanent tradeoff

The Taylor Rule

Q: How do central banks set the policy rate, in theory?

A:

  • \(i_{t} = r^{*}+\pi_{t}+0.5(\pi_{t}-\pi^{*})+0.5(y_{t}-y^{*})\)
  • \(r^{*}\): neutral real rate; \(\pi^{*}\): inflation target; \((y_{t}-y^{*})\): output gap
  • Raises rates when inflation runs above target or the economy runs hot; cuts rates when either runs below

The IS Curve

Q: What does the IS curve capture?

A:

  • The relationship between real output and the real interest rate
  • Higher real rates \(\rightarrow\) lower investment/consumption \(\rightarrow\) lower output (downward sloping)
  • Combined with a monetary policy rule, gives the “IS-MP” (or IS-LM) framework for jointly determining output and rates

Business Cycles

Q: What are the phases of a business cycle, and how long does one typically last?

A:

  • Phases: Expansion \(\rightarrow\) Peak \(\rightarrow\) Contraction (recession) \(\rightarrow\) Trough \(\rightarrow\) Expansion again
  • U.S. cycles have historically averaged roughly 5-6 years peak-to-peak, though duration is highly variable
  • The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee determines official U.S. turning points using a range of indicators, not a single GDP rule
  • Indicators are classified as Leading (e.g. yield curve, building permits), Coincident (e.g. industrial production, employment), or Lagging (e.g. unemployment duration, inflation)
